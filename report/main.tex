\begin{abstract}
General-purpose text-to-audio models such as AudioLDM and AudioGen produce
generic results when prompted with descriptions of niche, low-fidelity sound
domains. We study the problem of generating \emph{Minecraft}-style sound
effects from text and present a systematic comparison of six adaptation
strategies built on top of three foundation backbones (AudioLDM,
AudioLDM\,2, and AudioGen) plus two from-scratch transformer baselines.
Our primary contribution is a parameter-efficient LoRA fine-tune of Meta's
\texttt{audiogen-medium} (1.5\,B parameter autoregressive model over EnCodec
tokens) on a 325-clip augmented dataset built from the official Minecraft
Java Edition assets. We document the training recipe required for stable
convergence on AudioCraft (FP32 only, NaN-suppression on the delay-pattern
logits, LoRA targeted at \texttt{out\_proj}/\texttt{linear1}/\texttt{linear2}
because q/k/v are fused), and evaluate with FAD, KAD, an Inception-score
proxy, CLAP text--audio similarity, and spectral statistics. The LoRA
adapter (132\,M trainable parameters, 25 epochs) reduces FAD by 37\,\% and
KAD by 73\,\% relative to the vanilla AudioGen baseline on in-domain
prompts, while remaining capable of out-of-distribution multi-event prompts
of up to 12 seconds. Trained adapter weights, 38 generated audio samples,
and an interactive Streamlit demo are released with the code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text-conditioned generative audio models have advanced rapidly over the
past three years, from latent-diffusion approaches such as
AudioLDM\,\cite{liu2023audioldm} and AudioLDM\,2\,\cite{liu2024audioldm2}
to autoregressive token-based models such as
AudioGen\,\cite{kreuk2023audiogen} and MusicGen\,\cite{copet2024simple}.
These models are trained on broad open-domain corpora (AudioSet, Freesound,
licensed music) and produce convincing results for everyday acoustic
events. They are, however, demonstrably weak on niche stylized domains:
prompting for ``Minecraft zombie'' yields a generic groan rather than the
recognizable lo-fi 8\,kHz timbre of the game.

We pick \textit{Minecraft sound effects} as a representative low-resource,
stylistically distinctive target domain. The full official sound bank
contains only $\sim$200 short clips spanning hostile mobs, ambient cave
loops, footstep textures, combat, and damage events. The data is small
enough to make full fine-tuning infeasible yet large enough that
parameter-efficient adaptation is plausible. The central question of this
project is therefore: \emph{which combination of base architecture and
adaptation method most reliably transfers a pretrained text-to-audio model
into this small, distinctive domain?}

We make three contributions. (1) We pursue six adaptation strategies in
parallel and report which converge and which fail. (2) For the strategy
that worked best in practice---LoRA on AudioGen's autoregressive
language model---we describe the precise training recipe required to avoid
known instabilities in AudioCraft. (3) We provide a quantitative
comparison using FAD, KAD, an Inception-score proxy, CLAP, and spectral
features, and release the LoRA weights, generated samples, and an
interactive demo.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Text-to-audio backbones.}
AudioLDM\,\cite{liu2023audioldm} introduced latent diffusion conditioned on
CLAP\,\cite{wu2023clap} text embeddings; AudioLDM\,2\,\cite{liu2024audioldm2}
combines a GPT-2 prefix with diffusion over a learned audio latent.
AudioGen\,\cite{kreuk2023audiogen} replaces diffusion with an
autoregressive transformer over discrete EnCodec tokens
\cite{defossez2023encodec}, using a delay pattern across 4 codebooks at
50\,Hz. We use Meta's \texttt{facebook/audiogen-medium} (1.5\,B parameters)
distributed via the AudioCraft library\,\cite{audiocraft2023}.

\textbf{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning.}
LoRA\,\cite{hu2022lora} injects low-rank updates $\Delta W = BA$ into
selected linear layers, freezing the base model. It is the de facto
standard for adapting large pretrained models on domain data and reduces
trainable parameters by one to two orders of magnitude. We adopt LoRA on
both the AudioLDM\,2 UNet (Approach~1) and the AudioGen language model
(Approach~4); only the latter converged.

\textbf{Audio generation evaluation.}
Fr{\'e}chet Audio Distance (FAD)\,\cite{kilgour2019fad} computes a
2-Wasserstein distance between Gaussian fits to embeddings of generated
versus reference audio. Kernel Audio Distance (KAD) is the audio analogue
of Kernel Inception Distance, using a polynomial-kernel
MMD\,\cite{gretton2012kernel}. CLAP\,\cite{wu2023clap} provides a
text--audio cosine similarity score. We complement these with an
Inception-score proxy\,\cite{salimans2016improved} computed over CLAP
embeddings and per-clip spectral statistics (centroid, bandwidth, rolloff,
ZCR, RMS).

\section{Dataset}

\textbf{Source.} We pull the official Minecraft Java Edition 1.21.1 sound
bank from \texttt{InventivetalentDev/minecraft-assets}\,\cite{minecraftassets}
via \texttt{scripts/fetch\_minecraft\_assets.py}. The raw collection
contains 195 \texttt{.ogg} files spanning the categories shown in
Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} All clips are decoded to 16\,kHz mono float WAV,
trimmed at a 30\,dB silence threshold, and either looped or cropped to a
fixed 4\,s window. Action sequences (e.g.\ ``hurt$\to$death'') are produced
by concatenating clips of the same mob with a 0.7\,s gap; footstep cycles
use a 0.1\,s gap. Each clip is augmented with two
speed-perturbed copies ($0.85\times$ and $1.15\times$) to give 325 total
clips, which are split 277/48 (train/val) using a deterministic seed.

\textbf{Captions.} A simple template
\textit{``minecraft \{entity\} \{action\} sound effect''} is generated
automatically by \texttt{scripts/build\_manifest.py} from the folder
hierarchy. The manifest is then converted to AudioCraft's expected JSONL
format with one JSON sidecar per WAV by
\texttt{scripts/prepare\_audiogen\_data.py}, producing
\texttt{data/audiogen/\{train,val\}.jsonl}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Approaches Explored}

(Table~\ref{tab:approaches}). Approaches 1--3 fine-tune diffusion

Approaches~5--6 train a small transformer from scratch on the same
EnCodec tokenizer. Approach~4 is our primary contribution and is described
in detail below; the others provide context and negative results.

\subsection{AudioGen LoRA: Architecture}

AudioGen consists of (i) a frozen T5 text encoder, (ii) a frozen EnCodec
neural codec that maps a 16\,kHz waveform to 4 codebooks of 2048-vocabulary
tokens at 50\,Hz, and (iii) a 1.5\,B-parameter autoregressive transformer
language model that generates the codebook tokens conditioned on T5
embeddings via cross-attention. The LM uses a \emph{delay pattern} that
staggers the four codebooks across timesteps, which means the first and
last few positions are predicted from invalid (NaN) context.

We inject LoRA adapters\,\cite{hu2022lora} of rank $r=128$ and scaling
$\alpha=256$ (effective scaling $\alpha/r = 2$) with dropout $0.05$ into
three projection layers per transformer block:
\texttt{out\_proj} (post-attention output), \texttt{linear1}, and
\texttt{linear2} (the two linears of the feed-forward block). We
deliberately do \emph{not} target the q/k/v projections: AudioCraft fuses
them into a single \texttt{in\_proj\_weight} tensor inside its
\texttt{StreamingMultiheadAttention}, so they cannot be cleanly wrapped by
the standard LoRA recipe. The total trainable count is
$\approx$132\,M parameters ($\approx$8.8\,\% of the base model).

\subsection{AudioGen LoRA: Training Recipe}

The training script
\texttt{src/mcaudio/train/audiogen\_lora\_train.py} implements the
following loop:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Load a batch of waveforms via the \texttt{McAudioDataset} class and
collate-pad to the maximum length in the batch.
\item Encode to discrete codes with the frozen EnCodec
($\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{Z}^{B\times 4\times T}$, $T$=200 for 4\,s).
\item Build \texttt{ConditioningAttributes} from the captions, dropping
each caption to the empty string with probability 0.1
(classifier-free-guidance dropout).
\item Call \texttt{lm.compute\_predictions(codes, conditions)} to obtain
logits of shape $(B, 4, T, 2048)$.
\item Apply \texttt{logits.nan\_to\_num(nan=0.0)} and compute masked
cross-entropy only on positions valid under the delay pattern's mask.
\item Backpropagate, accumulate gradients across 4 steps, then step the
AdamW optimizer (lr $3{\times}10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, cosine
schedule with 50-step warmup).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{FP32 only.} We disable mixed precision entirely. AudioCraft's
delay-pattern arithmetic produces NaNs under \texttt{torch.autocast} that
silently corrupt gradients. The combination of FP32 weights and the
\texttt{nan\_to\_num} clamp on the logits is the minimal recipe that
trains stably; we observed immediate divergence under bf16 or fp16
autocast even with the same NaN clamp.

\textbf{Hyperparameters.} Effective batch size 8 (micro-batch 2,
gradient-accumulation 4), 25 epochs (the value used for the reported
metrics; the configuration default of 150 epochs offers minimal additional
gain), checkpoints saved every 5 epochs plus a ``best by validation loss''
adapter. Each adapter checkpoint is approximately 176\,MB on disk; the
frozen base model is shared. Training completes in roughly one hour on a
single RTX\,5090 (32\,GB) and is also feasible on a Colab T4 with reduced
batch size.

\subsection{Inference}

For generation we reload the base AudioGen model, re-inject empty LoRA
adapters of the same shape, then load the saved adapter tensors and cast
them to the base-model dtype on-device. Sampling parameters mirror the
official AudioGen demo: top-$k$ 250, temperature 0.9, classifier-free
guidance coefficient 3.0, duration 4--12\,s.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Protocol}

We compare three audio populations against the 325-clip processed
reference set:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{baseline}: 8 clips from vanilla AudioGen on four in-domain
prompts (zombie hurt, skeleton death, cave ambience, stone footsteps).
\item \textbf{lora}: the same prompts re-rendered through the LoRA
adapter.
\item \textbf{generalization}: 22 clips (11 vanilla, 11 LoRA) on
out-of-distribution multi-event prompts up to 12\,s
(e.g.\ ``creeper hiss then explosion, player hurt sound'';
``cave ambience with water dripping and distant zombie groan'').
\end{itemize}
All metrics are computed by
\texttt{presentation/compute\_metrics\_v2.py} and stored in
\texttt{presentation/metrics.json}.

Table~\ref{tab:main-metrics} summarises the headline results. On the
in-domain prompts the LoRA adapter reduces FAD by 37\,\% and KAD by 73\,\%
relative to vanilla AudioGen; mean CLAP also improves from 0.142 to 0.162.

set (FAD $4263\to 2820$, KAD $1.19{\times}10^8\to 3.41{\times}10^7$),

rather than memorising the training captions.

The Inception-score proxy is essentially flat in-domain (4.00 vs.\ 4.00
with $k=4$ KMeans clusters; the proxy saturates because we only generate 8
samples per condition) and slightly lower for LoRA on the OOD set, which
is consistent with the LoRA model concentrating its mass on the learned
domain manifold. CLAP similarity drops slightly on OOD prompts after LoRA
(0.341$\to$0.270): this is the expected trade-off of any specialisation
step---outputs are more Minecraft-like but the text encoder, trained on
generic AudioSet captions, no longer scores them as cleanly aligned.

\subsection{Comparison Across Approaches}

Approach~1 (LoRA on the AudioLDM\,2 UNet cross-attention) produced
unstructured noise after 100--500 optimizer steps; we attribute the
failure to AudioLDM\,2's heterogeneous architecture (CLAP\,+\,T5\,+\,GPT-2
prefix\,+\,UNet\,+\,VAE\,+\,vocoder) which requires coordinated adaptation
across components rather than a single localized adapter. Full fine-tuning
of the AudioLDM\,1 UNet (Approach~2, 3 epochs) was numerically stable but
produced only marginal qualitative improvement; the v2 expanded-dataset
run (Approach~3, 50 epochs / 17K steps on 556 clips) converged but did
not reach the FAD level of the AudioGen LoRA. The from-scratch
transformer baselines (Approaches~5--6, 25\,M parameters, 50 and 100
epochs) produce intelligible Minecraft-textured output but lack the
prompt-following ability of any of the pretrained backbones.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why does AudioGen LoRA work where AudioLDM\,2 LoRA fails?}
Two factors stand out. First, autoregressive next-token cross-entropy
provides a much sharper per-position learning signal than the noise-MSE
used in latent diffusion training, which is particularly important when
the dataset is small. Second, the EnCodec discrete token space is already
band-limited to the 16\,kHz tokenization grid, which happens to match
Minecraft's lo-fi character; the diffusion latent of AudioLDM\,2 instead
encodes mel-bin reconstructions tuned for full-bandwidth audio.

\textbf{The LoRA / generalization trade-off.} The OOD CLAP drop
($0.341\to0.270$) is a direct measurement of the cost of specialisation.
A practitioner facing this trade-off has two levers: (i) reduce LoRA rank
or train for fewer epochs to stay closer to the base model's
generalisation behaviour, or (ii) keep the LoRA at its current strength
and apply it as a switchable adapter (load only when Minecraft-style
output is desired). We adopt the second approach in our demo by exposing
both the vanilla and LoRA-adapted models side-by-side.

\textbf{Limitations.} Our evaluation is limited by a small reference set
(325 clips total, 48 in validation), which inflates the variance of
distributional metrics; the absolute FAD/KAD numbers should be read as
\emph{relative} comparisons within this study rather than as
cross-paper-comparable scores. We also did not run formal listening tests.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a domain-specialised text-to-audio system for Minecraft sound
effects. After comparing six adaptation strategies, a 132\,M-parameter
LoRA adapter on AudioGen's autoregressive LM emerged as the clear winner,
reducing FAD by 37\,\% and KAD by 73\,\% versus the vanilla model on
in-domain prompts and improving them further on out-of-distribution
multi-event prompts. The training recipe required to achieve this on
AudioCraft is tightly constrained---FP32 weights, NaN-clamped logits,
LoRA on \texttt{out\_proj}/\texttt{linear1}/\texttt{linear2} only---and we
document it in full alongside the released adapter weights, generated
samples, and an interactive demo.
